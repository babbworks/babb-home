Two days on a feed-lot in Sioux Falls.

Workpads field crew rides the third shift with the operations lead at a 4,200-head feed-lot on the Big Sioux. Pen rotations, weather windows, and the small arithmetic that keeps the place running.

By Reporter Name · with Photographer Name · Edited by Editor A

The sun is not up when Cal walks the south pens. He has a clipboard he does not use, a head-lamp he turns off, and a Workpad in a pouch on his belt that he taps without looking. Pen 14 is fine. Pen 15 is fine. Pen 16 he stops at, leans on the rail for a minute, then taps three times and moves on. He is the operations lead here, and what he is doing — mostly — is counting.

The lot sits north of Sioux Falls, between the river and a two-lane that carries trucks all day. Four thousand two hundred head, give or take a daily handful, in forty-eight pensGlossary · feed-lotA "pen" is a fenced area holding a group of cattle on a single feeding schedule. Pen counts vary by lot; 48 is on the smaller end for the region., on three rotations. The crew is twelve people across two shifts. The math is plain and the weather makes it interesting.

I am here for two days at the invitation of the lot and with a Workpad field crew, who are not running the place — they are watching how it is run. The product is two years old. The lot has been here forty-one.

§ IIPens & rotation

Pens & rotation

The first thing Cal does, every morning, is decide which pens go to which feed truck. The decision looks instant from outside — he names them in order, the driver writes them down — but underneath it is a string of small judgments about who ate yesterday, who didn’t, who got moved on Tuesday and shouldn’t get moved again until Friday.

Before the Workpad it lived on paper, then in a spreadsheet, then in a different spreadsheet. Now it lives in a structured form with three required fields and one optional photo. The required fields are not the interesting part. The optional photo is.

You can put anything in the notes field. The photo is what makes me trust the notes.
— Cal, operations lead · Sioux Falls feed-lot

Three things have to be true for a system like this to land on a lot like this one. It has to work with gloves on. It has to work with no signal. And it has to print something at the end of the day that the owner, who is seventy-two, can read at his kitchen table.

The first morning we lose forty minutes to a gate latch on Pen 22. It is the kind of thing that does not show up in any data set. It also does not show up in any product roadmap. We fix it with a length of haywire and the second day’s plan gets pushed up an hour.

§ IIIThe crew

The crew

There are twelve people working the lot across day and night, and four of them have been there longer than the owner’s youngest son has been alive. The others are newer — including two who came over from a beef plant that closed in February — and the handoff between the two groups is, as far as I can tell, the hardest unsolved problem on the property.

Workpads’ bet, on lots like this, is that a structured handoff — the same five questions every shift, the same three photos every Friday — is worth more than any dashboard. The lot’s bet is that the field crew, watching them run their place, will leave it better than they found it.

I want to report that those two bets are converging. They are, mostly. They are also a product in beta meeting a place built in 1985, and there are mornings where the better tool is still a length of haywire.

§ IVThe math

The math

The number on the wall of the owner’s office is $0.18. That is what a pound of gain costs to put on a steer at this lot, give or take, on a good week. A bad week is $0.22. A really bad week is $0.27 and you stop printing the number.

What the Workpad is for — the actual point of it, after everything else — is making sure that the number on the wall is real. Not optimized, not predicted. Real. Counted. Photographed.

This is a small thing, said plainly, and it is the entire job.

§ VLeaving

Leaving

On the second afternoon the wind comes up off the river and we lose another hour to a sun-shade panel on Pen 31. We do not fix it with haywire this time. Cal taps three times, says the truck can come tomorrow, and goes back to the office to print the day’s rotation for the morning crew.

The Workpad prints to a small label-roll on the corner of his desk. The morning crew reads it the way the morning crew reads everything: standing up, with coffee, in a hurry. Then they go out to the pens and start counting.

Two days is not enough time to know a place. It is enough time to know what to come back for.
