Tony R.

Foreman at Hamilton steel mill · third shift · 30 years

Came up through the second shift in 1996, did a year on the first, then settled on the third when his nephew came on as an apprentice. Speaks plainly, walks slowly, knows which gates to close before lightning storms. The subject of the 38-minute interview and the voice that anchors The Pour.

A. Where he came from

Tony R. was born and raised on the east end of Hamilton, four blocks from the gate his father walked through every morning for thirty-two years. He started at the mill at twenty — finishing-line scrap on the second shift, $9.20 an hour, the year the Russians invaded Chechnya. He came up through every position on the line that anyone would let him try. He was never a foreman’s son; he became a foreman because the foreman he worked under retired in ’14 and the people who reported to him asked the GM to keep things going the way they were.

He has worked all three shifts. He left the floor exactly twice — for the birth of each of his children, six and four days respectively. He took the third shift on permanently in 2018, when his nephew came on as an apprentice and asked, point-blank, to learn from him. He is now responsible for the mill from 23:00 to 07:00 every weekday and every other Saturday.

"You learn the floor when you stop expecting it to behave. The floor doesn’t behave. The floor is fine. You have to be fine with the floor." — from the interview, 01:08

B. What he does on a shift

Tony R. runs the third shift. That means he’s responsible for the four panels — charge, blow, tap, cast — the crew of eighteen who work them, the conversation with the second-shift foreman who hands off to him at 23:00, and the conversation with the first-shift foreman who he hands off to at 07:00. He carries the rolled-up paper schedule in his back pocket and a Workpads tablet in his right hand. He prefers the paper.

Most of the shift is uneventful. Most shifts at most mills are uneventful, and people who report on mills tend to write the eventful ones because they make better copy. The Babb posture on this Place is that the uneventful shift is the point.

C. What he is the subject of

Tony R. has agreed to be the subject of recurring coverage at the Hamilton steel mill standing story. That means: ongoing interviews, photography, occasional audio captures during shifts (with the floor’s permission), and the right of refusal on any photograph or quote before publication. Consent envelopes signed under BABB-FIELD 0.2; on file with the editor.

He is also the voice on The Pour, the foreman in the dispatch, and the subject of the long interview. Six of fourteen field notes anchor to something he said or did. If a journalist or photographer asks to spend time with him, contact the editor first.
